How the Simulation Works

Boids Algorithm

The term "boids" was coined by Craig Reynolds in 1986 to describe his algorithm for simulating the flocking behavior of birds. The same principles apply remarkably well to ground-based herding animals like goats and sheep. The simulation creates complex, lifelike group behavior from just a few simple rules that each individual follows independently.

This implementation adapts the classic boids algorithm specifically for ground animals, incorporating realistic constraints such as limited field of view (270 degrees - goats cannot see directly behind themselves), angle-based orientation, and smooth turning mechanics that reflect the physical limitations of quadrupedal movement.

Core Behavioral Rules

The simulation operates on three fundamental rules that govern how each goat moves in relation to its neighbors:

  • Separation: Goats maintain personal space and actively avoid crowding their immediate neighbors. When another goat gets too close (within 35 pixels), a repulsion force is applied, causing the goat to turn away and speed up slightly. This prevents collisions and maintains realistic spacing within the herd.
  • Alignment: Each goat attempts to match the heading of nearby goats it can see. By averaging the direction of visible neighbors within a 120-pixel visual range, goats naturally synchronize their movement, creating the flowing, coordinated motion characteristic of real herds.
  • Cohesion: Goats are drawn toward the center of their visible neighbors, preventing the herd from dispersing. This social attraction keeps isolated individuals moving back toward the group while maintaining the overall herd structure.

Vision and Field of View

Unlike simpler boid implementations, this simulation incorporates a realistic field of view system. Each goat has a 270-degree vision cone extending forward and to the sides, but cannot see directly behind itself. This creates more natural behavior where goats may temporarily lose track of herd mates behind them, leading to interesting emergent patterns as they turn to rejoin the group.

Additionally, the simulation includes line-of-sight blocking by boundary fences. Goats cannot see through the enclosure walls, which affects their cohesion and alignment calculations. This creates realistic behavior near boundaries where goats may spread out more than in open areas of the enclosure.

Movement Mechanics

The goats move with angle-based physics that simulate realistic quadrupedal locomotion. Each animal has a heading (direction) and speed, with constraints on how quickly they can turn (maximum turn rate of 0.12 radians per frame). This prevents unrealistic instant direction changes and creates smooth, arc-like turning patterns.

Speed is variable and contextual: goats accelerate when avoiding threats or chasing treats, slow down after fence collisions, and experience continuous friction that brings them back to a natural cruising speed. A forward bias parameter encourages goats to maintain their current heading, reducing jittery behavior and creating more purposeful movement.

Boundary Behavior

The simulation implements a sophisticated two-layer boundary system. A soft boundary (100 pixels from the edge) applies gentle turning forces that encourage goats to head back toward the center. A hard boundary (20 pixels from the edge) acts as an impenetrable fence, clamping position to prevent any crossing.

User-drawn fences use segment intersection detection. When a goat's movement path intersects with a fence, the collision is detected and the goat's heading is reflected across the fence angle, similar to a ball bouncing off a wall. The goat is also nudged away from the fence slightly and its speed is reduced, creating a realistic bouncing behavior.

Interactive Features

The simulation includes several interactive elements that demonstrate behavioral flexibility:

  • Treat Mode: Moving the mouse creates an attractant that goats will move toward, accelerating as they approach. The effect diminishes with distance (up to 200 pixels) and can influence large portions of the herd, creating waves of movement.
  • Avoid Mode: Pressing spacebar toggles to avoidance mode, where the mouse cursor becomes a threat. Goats will flee from the cursor, with even stronger acceleration than in attract mode, simulating a panic response.
  • Fence Drawing: Click and drag to draw custom fences. Goats will collide with and bounce off these barriers, and they also block line of sight, affecting herd cohesion calculations on either side of the fence.

Emergent Behavior

The beauty of the boids algorithm lies in emergence - complex group behaviors that arise from simple individual rules. In this simulation, you may observe herds splitting and merging around obstacles, wave-like patterns propagating through the group in response to disturbances, and natural-looking swirling patterns as goats negotiate the competing forces of separation, alignment, and cohesion.

These patterns closely mirror observations of real goat and sheep herds, demonstrating how sophisticated collective behavior does not require centralized coordination or complex individual intelligence. Each goat operates independently with no knowledge of the overall herd structure, yet the group exhibits coordinated, purposeful movement.

Technical Implementation

The simulation runs in JavaScript using HTML5 Canvas for rendering. Each frame, every goat calculates behavioral forces (separation, alignment, cohesion, boundary avoidance, and treat response) which modify its angular velocity. After applying turn rate limits and forward bias, the heading is updated and used to calculate the new position based on current speed.

Fence collision detection uses line segment intersection tests, comparing the goat's movement vector against all fence segments. The simulation maintains 60 frames per second performance with 400 goats through efficient neighbor queries (vision-limited rather than checking all pairs) and optimized trigonometric calculations.

This simulation demonstrates principles from Craig Reynolds' original boids algorithm (1986), adapted for ground-based herding animals with realistic vision and movement constraints.
